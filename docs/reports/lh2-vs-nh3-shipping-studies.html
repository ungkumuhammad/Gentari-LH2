LH2 vs Ammonia at Sea

Both ships have to move themselves as well as their cargo. The ammonia carrier burns 25 t/day of VLSFO and re-liquefies its boil-off. The hydrogen carrier burns its own cargo instead — so the real question is whether that boil-off covers the fuel bill, falls short of it, or overshoots it, and what the hydrogen given up is worth.

Does the boil-off cover the fuel bill?

Sweeping: LH₂ boil-off rate

Kawasaki says the carrier's fuel-gas consumption equals its boil-off rate. That only closes if the boil-off happens to match what the engine wants. Below the demand line the ship still buys bunker fuel; above it, boil-off arrives faster than the engine can burn it and the excess leaves the ship having done no work at all.

LH₂ boil-off vs. ammonia as a lower-CI marine fuel

Sweeping: LH₂ boil-off rate

A different way to cut this vessel's carbon intensity: instead of buying VLSFO, run the ammonia carrier's engine on its own cargo — 100% ammonia, with a small pilot-fuel share still needed to sustain combustion (5% of the energy by default, a live input). Ammonia carries no carbon, so this is a genuine lower-CI configuration for the ship itself — the trade is bunkering roughly twice the mass, because ammonia's energy density is under half that of VLSFO. Both fuels are converted to the same VLSFO-equivalent basis so they compare directly against LH₂'s own boil-off.

What does the boil-off cost, once hydrogen is priced as a premium fuel?

Sweeping: VLSFO price

This is the comparison that matters, put on a fuel-to-fuel basis: NH₃'s re-liquefaction fuel against LH₂'s boil-off, valued as the fuel it displaces — both priced off the same swept VLSFO price rather than an arbitrary hydrogen value. What is not sourced here

  1. LH₂ voyage boil-off rate. Kawasaki disclosed none — only that fuel-gas consumption equals the boil-off rate. 0.2 %/day is a placeholder; the nearest public figure for a comparable vessel (RSER, 2026) is ~3.44 %/day. Everything on this page turns on it.
  2. The LH₂ carrier's propulsion duty is the weakest number on this page. 25 t/day is your figure for the 24,000 m³ ammonia vessel, carried across so both ships share one basis — but a 160,000 m³ hull at ~16 kn would realistically burn several times that. It is a live input for exactly this reason: at ~100 t/day (roughly proportionate to the 4× cargo) the boil-off covers about 64 % of demand and the surplus disappears entirely. The venting finding below is largely an artifact of the 25 t/day figure — set it to something real before quoting any of it.
  3. Hydrogen and bunker prices are yours to set. Neither is logged in the repository, and no default is asserted as correct — the study reports the breakeven price instead.
  4. Every ammonia vessel figure — speed, capacity, port time, boil-off, re-liquefaction energy, genset efficiency — is user-stated or estimated, pending the internal Gentari NH₃ dataset (decision D4). The 24k / 40k / 60k / 90k scenario sizes and their class labels (mid-size, large, VLGC) are general industry size bands, not figures from this repository.
  5. Propulsion duty does not transfer between hulls. The stated 25 t/day belongs to the 24,000 m³ ammonia vessel. Each duty input carries a size^⅔ suggestion scaled from that pair (resistance ≈ wetted area), offered as a one-click "use" rather than applied silently — at 90,000 m³ that is ≈60 t/day, and for the 160,000 m³ LH₂ hull ≈89 t/day. No power curve is held for any of these vessels, so the scaling is a rule of thumb, not a specification.
  6. Surplus disposal. The page defaults to venting, per your framing. Kawasaki's own reply Q26 describes a gas combustion unit burning excess boil-off above MARVS — in which case the hydrogen is oxidised to water and the warming figure does not apply. The hydrogen is lost from the cargo either way; only the environmental line changes. Toggle it in the detail panel.
  7. Vented-hydrogen warming potential (11.6 kg CO₂e/kg, 100-year) is an estimate with a wide published uncertainty band and no primary citation logged.
  8. Ballast leg excluded for both vessels. Propulsion fuel is charged over the laden leg only, where the boil-off is generated. Including it would raise both carriers' fuel bills and dilute the LH₂ carrier's displacement credit.
  9. VLSFO LHV (40.2 MJ/kg) is the marine convention for residual fuel, not a citation from the repository's reference registry.

Liquefy It, or React It

The same 100 ktpa of hydrogen arrives at the fence as a gas. One route chills it to 20 K; the other reacts it with nitrogen into ammonia. Liquefaction only ever spends energy. Synthesis spends energy and gives some back — the Haber–Bosch reaction is exothermic, and that heat can raise steam and turn a turbine. This sheet sizes both routes in megawatts, then puts the same 60,000 m³ tank behind each of them.

How the 60,000 m³ instruction is read here. Both terminals are given the same tank volume, so the comparison is per unit of installed storage — not the same days of cover, and not the same stored energy. That reading matters: at equal volume the ammonia tank holds more hydrogen and covers more days, so equalising volume is not a neutral choice. The second reading — equal days of cover — is a toggle in the controls, and it reverses which side needs the bigger tank. For scale, Kawasaki's own LH₂ export tank is 64,000 m³ (kawasaki-2026-supplemental), so 60,000 m³ is within a few percent of a real LH₂ design.

How much can the steam turbine claw back?

Sweeping: heat → power recovery

The synthesis reaction releases a fixed amount of heat per kilogram of hydrogen it consumes. What varies is how much of that heat is raised as usable steam and how efficiently the steam cycle converts it. Their product is the only lever on this chart — and it has a hard ceiling, because no Rankine cycle turns heat into work at better than about a third. This chart is a standalone hypothetical: every other number on this page — verdict tiles, the power-balance chart above, the ledger, and the Overall Value Chain sheet — charges the ammonia route its gross demand, with no steam-turbine credit at all.

When does terminal boil-off start to matter?

Sweeping: LH₂ tank boil-off rate

Boil-off dominates the shipping sheet. Onshore it does not, and it is worth showing why rather than asserting it: the tank holds days of production, not months, and the re-liquefier only ever sees what boils off — never the whole stream. Measured against the plant it sits next to, the terminal is a rounding error until the boil-off rate is many times what Kawasaki quotes.

What is not sourced here

  1. The Haber–Bosch electrical demand is the number that decides this sheet, and it is a placeholder. 0.60 kWh/kg NH₃ (range 0.5–0.7) is an [ESTIMATE] in data/carriers/chain-energy-defaults.csv, pending the internal Gentari NH₃ dataset (decision D4). It is stated to cover the air-separation unit, syngas compression, the synthesis loop and loop refrigeration — but that boundary has never been confirmed. The parity map exists so the answer can be read against how wrong this figure might be.
  2. The steam-turbine credit is excluded from every number on this page, by user direction (2026-09-23). The reaction heat itself is solid — 45.9 kJ/mol NH₃, the same enthalpy the repository already carries for the reverse (cracking) reaction, giving 4.22 kWh per kg of H₂ reacted. What was previously assumed on top of that first-principles quantity — how much of it survives as usable steam (75%) and what a steam cycle makes of that (30%) — is no longer applied anywhere in the main comparison; the ammonia route is always charged its gross conversion electricity. Part of the original motivation for excluding it by default still holds as a documented risk: if the 0.60 kWh/kg Haber–Bosch figure is already a net number for a plant with steam drives, crediting it again would have double-counted the saving. The "How much can the steam turbine claw back?" study still explores the credit as a standalone hypothetical, with its own inputs, entirely separate from the rest of this sheet.
  3. Product refrigeration to −33 °C is not modelled separately. Ammonia leaves the synthesis loop warm and pressurised and must be let down to atmospheric refrigerated storage. The 0.60 kWh/kg row claims to include loop refrigeration; whether it covers the let-down to tank conditions is not stated. If it does not, the ammonia route's demand here is understated.
  4. Every ammonia terminal figure — density, boil-off rate, re-liquefaction energy — is an estimate pending the same internal dataset. The LH₂ side's 0.1 %/day and 64,000 m³ tank are Kawasaki-disclosed (kawasaki-2026-supplemental); the 3.94 kWh/kg BOG re-liquefaction energy is derived, not disclosed — it scales the main liquefier's SEC by the ratio of condensation-only ideal work to fresh-feed ideal work.
  5. Neither CapEx nor OpEx appears anywhere on this sheet. Kawasaki declined both ("Feasibility Study necessary", replies Q8/Q9), so a liquefaction cost per kg cannot be stated without fabricating it. This sheet ranks the two routes on energy only. A route that draws less power is not automatically the cheaper one — an ammonia chain still has to pay for a cracker at the far end, which this boundary excludes entirely.
  6. Plant operating hours (8,000 h/y) set the megawatt figures but not the energy ones. It is an assumption, not a Kawasaki figure; the terminal is charged over all 8,760 h because a tank boils off whether or not the plant runs.
  7. The theoretical minimum liquefaction work — 3.9 kWh/kg on the para-hydrogen basis, 3.3 kWh/kg without ortho–para conversion (doe-2009-h2-liquefaction-energy, cited) — assumes an ambient-pressure gaseous feed. A pressurised feed lowers it. The floor drawn on these charts is therefore an upper bound on the floor.
  8. Hydrogen production is outside the boundary. It is common to both routes and cancels. For scale only: at the repository's 60 kWh/kg electrolysis placeholder it would be roughly 750 MW — several times either conversion step, and the reason neither of these numbers is the whole story.

Regasify It, or Crack It

The same 100 ktpa of hydrogen arrives at the fence as a liquid or a molecule. One route warms it back to a gas against seawater; the other burns natural gas to tear ammonia apart. Regasification spends almost no purchased energy. Cracking spends real fuel and emits real carbon — the mirror image of the upstream sheet, where synthesis gave heat back and liquefaction only ever took it. This sheet puts the same 60,000 m³ tank behind each receiving terminal and sizes both routes in power, carbon and cost.

What is not sourced here

  1. Every cracker figure is scaled from KBR's disclosed 80 ktpa case, not a 100 ktpa design. KBR states H2ACT® performance is consistent "across different modes of operation and the scale range," but 100 ktpa was never itself quoted — the four disclosed capacities top out at 80 ktpa H₂. Electricity, ammonia ratio and direct CI are carried forward unscaled; CAPEX, which KBR shows scaling well below linearly (78 → 209 MMUSD across 12 → 80 ktpA), is not shown on this sheet at all.
  2. Natural-gas duty is no longer read directly from KBR — it is derived from a standardised total thermal-duty budget. The 80 ktpa Clean Fuel case (7.20 t NH₃/t H₂, zero external NG) is read as the 100%-self-fuelled anchor: its excess ammonia over the 5.632 stoichiometric ratio (1.568 t NH₃-equiv/t H₂) is taken to be the entire cracker thermal duty, expressed via NH₃'s own LHV (5.167 kWh/kg, itself an [ESTIMATE]). Every other ammonia-feed input then splits that same fixed duty between self-fuelled cracked ammonia and purchased NG. This makes the two fuel sources move together with one input (as requested), but it assumes KBR's 100% NG (6.35 t/t) and 50% NG (6.72 t/t) cases sit on the same total-duty line as the Clean Fuel case — checking the 100% NG point against this model implies roughly 4.4 kWh/kg H₂ of NG duty, about 11% above the value implied by directly reading the 12 ktpa heat & material balance's NG:H₂ mass flow ratio (0.2914 kg/kg → ~3.96 kWh/kg). That gap is plausibly real — KBR's own disclosed "Energy Efficiency (LHV)" differs slightly by firing mode (89.8% at 100% NG vs 88.7% at Clean Fuel, 80 ktpa) — but it means this sheet's NG figures at the 100%/50% NG presets no longer reproduce the HMB's own mass-flow figure exactly; they are self-consistent with each other, not independently re-verified against it.
  3. NG LHV (48.9 MJ/kg) is not a KBR figure. It was originally back-solved to reproduce KBR's disclosed direct CI at the 100% NG point via an IPCC 2006 default combustion factor (56.1 kg CO₂/GJ); it is now used only to convert the derived NG thermal-energy figure back into a mass (kt/y) for display, and no longer feeds the CI calculation at all (CI now comes straight from KBR's disclosed per-firing-mode figure, id-ci).
  4. Ammonia fed beyond the Clean Fuel basis (>7.20 t/t) is not modelled as displacing anything further. The sheet flags it ("beyond 100% self-fuelling") and still charges its full cost via the yield-loss tile, but treats any NG requirement as already at zero — it cannot go negative.
  5. The ammonia terminal boil-off rate (0.05 %/day) is a user-set assumption, not a citation. What this summary can and can't tell you

  1. There is no honest total chain cost. Kawasaki declined to disclose liquefaction CapEx or OpEx ("Feasibility Study necessary") and the ammonia synthesis side has no cost figure either — only its electrical energy is modelled. Summing shipping and downstream cost while silently omitting upstream would understate whichever route has the larger hidden upstream cost; this sheet shows the two disclosed cost segments side by side instead and says plainly that upstream's piece is missing from both.
  2. Two different energy totals live on this sheet, on purpose. The "electrical energy" chart further down counts grid electricity only, upstream + downstream, because that is the one energy carrier genuinely common to both segments. The "total energy required" chart at the top of this sheet is broader and answers a different question — it adds electricity, purchased VLSFO, and hydrogen the LH₂ carrier burns or vents from its own cargo, all converted to kWh (this artifact's standard energy unit throughout), because for an energy-only comparison (not a cost or emissions one) it is legitimate to sum different energy carriers on a common heat-value basis. Do not read the two charts as disagreeing; they are scoped differently and say so in their own headings.
  3. The total-energy chart rescales upstream for the LH₂ route, not the NH₃ route, and says why. Producing 1 kg of hydrogen that actually survives the voyage requires liquefying slightly more than 1 kg, because some of what leaves the liquefier boils off and never reaches the customer — the Shipping sheet's own boil-off physics (evaluate()'s loss fraction) is used to scale upstream's LH₂ figure up by that same amount. The ammonia route needs no equivalent correction because its boil-off is re-liquefied and returned to the cargo (mass conserved) — the cost this imposes instead is the VLSFO its genset burns, already counted in its shipping bar. This chart does not apply an analogous correction to ammonia's upstream figure for the extra ammonia the cracker burns as fuel beyond the pure stoichiometric ratio (KBR's disclosed feed ratios run above 5.632 t/t) — Haber–Bosch's own sheet is sized only to the stoichiometric requirement, so that fuel-ammonia's own upstream production energy is not represented anywhere on this sheet. This likely understates the ammonia route's true total somewhat; by how much depends on the firing mode selected on the Inputs sheet.
  4. Ambient heat is deliberately excluded. LH₂ regasification's heat duty (id-regasheat, shown in the ledger for reference) comes free from ambient seawater and draws no primary energy, unlike the cracker's purchased natural gas or self-fuelled ammonia — including it in the "energy required" total would overstate what the LH₂ route actually has to produce or buy.
  5. Carbon intensity is downstream-complete, upstream-partial, shipping-absent. Downstream's CI (direct NG combustion + indirect grid electricity) is a full segment figure. Upstream's CI shown here is derived for this summary only — its own sheet reports MW and kWh/kg, not CO₂ — by multiplying upstream's electrical draw by the same shared grid CI assumption used downstream; it is not a citation, and upstream's own sheet does not carry this line. Shipping has no CI figure for the NH₃ side and only a conditional vented-hydrogen warming-potential estimate for LH₂ (shown on the Shipping sheet, not summed in here).
  6. Every figure updates from the same shared inputs (H₂ capacity, electricity price, grid CI, densities, terminal tank basis/volume, BOR, BOG re-liquefaction energy) now consolidated on the Inputs sheet, plus each segment's own sheet-specific assumptions. Change either and this sheet recomputes with everything else.
  7. The "more segments/metrics favour" scorecard is a count, not a weighted verdict. It treats each disclosed, comparable metric equally; it does not know that, say, cost might matter more to a final investment decision than carbon intensity, or that a bigger energy gap should outweigh a smaller cost gap. Read the individual tiles, not just the scorecard.
