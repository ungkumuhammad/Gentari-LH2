Why is the smaller ship competitive at all?

Fixed: one voyage

A 24,000 m³ hull against a 40,000 m³ hull looks like a mismatch until you follow the mass. Liquid ammonia is roughly ten times denser than liquid hydrogen, and it takes 5.632 kg of it to carry 1 kg of H₂.

Which carrier pays more energy to move a kilogram?

Sweeping: distance · two currencies

The two ships pay for boil-off in different currencies, and this study keeps them separate rather than adding them up. The LH₂ carrier burns its own cargo — hydrogen leaves as propulsion fuel. The ammonia carrier re-liquefies, spending electricity but losing no cargo.

What is not sourced here

  1. LH₂ voyage boil-off rate. KHI disclosed none. 0.2 %/day is a placeholder; the nearest public figure for a comparable vessel class (RSER, 2026) is ~3.44 %/day and is itself that paper's synthesis of a third-party study. Studies 2 and 3 exist because of this gap.
  2. Every ammonia vessel figure. Speed, capacity, port time, boil-off rate and re-liquefaction energy are user-stated or estimated, pending the internal Gentari NH₃ dataset (decision D4). None is vendor-confirmed.
  3. Corridor distance. Both routings are first-principles great-circle waypoint sums, not AIS or route-planner output — sea-distance services are unreachable from this environment. Cross-checked to within 2 % against a public Karachi→Hamburg figure.
  4. Propulsion energy is not modelled for either ship. LH₂ boil-off burned as fuel displaces marine gas oil the vessel would otherwise consume, so Study 5's LH₂ line is a gross cargo-energy figure, not a net penalty. Reading it as "wasted" overstates the case against LH₂.
  5. No cost. Vessel CapEx, day-rates and OpEx are logged for neither carrier, so nothing here is a $/kg answer.
  6. Ammonia density and LHV are standard published values with no primary citation in the repository's reference registry yet.
